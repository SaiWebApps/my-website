Who Trains the Next Senior? Managing Software Engineering 
When AI Makes Code Cheap
Gajendra Babu Thokala (Senior Engineering Leader; Fellow, British Computer Society)
Sairam B. Krishnan (Senior Engineering Leader; Wharton Executive MBA)
Abstract
Generative and agentic AI have made the routine work of software engineering nearly free, and 
the first casualty is the junior engineer. Engineering leaders now face a choice most are making 
by default. Cheap machine-made code commoditizes breadth, the plausible and conventional, 
while raising the value of two things humans still supply and machines do not: depth, the 
judgment of which answer is right in this system, and divergence, the perspective from outside 
the consensus that a senior-plus-AI team structurally lacks. Left to inertia, organizations slide 
into substitution, replacing juniors and letting review, apprenticeship, and the diversity of what 
they build hollow out. The alternative, complementarity, is a set of concrete moves: shrink the 
unit of review, tier scrutiny by consequence, measure verified outcomes rather than raw output, 
and rebuild the apprenticeship on purpose. We show why the choice is real, what each path costs, 
and what to do about it now.
Keywords: generative AI; agentic AI; software engineering; code review; apprenticeship; engineering 
management; human capital
1. Introduction
Every craft once ran on apprenticeship. The mason did not begin by designing the cathedral; he 
began by cutting stone under someone who had cut a great deal of it, and somewhere in the years 
of cutting he learned which stone would hold and which would betray the wall a decade later. 
The apprentice was cheap labor, but the labor was also the lesson, and you could not separate the 
two. The work the novice did was the curriculum through which he became a master.
Software engineering kept a version of that bargain longer than most professions. Junior 
engineers wrote the boilerplate, implemented the well-specified ticket, fixed the small bug, and 
stood up the new service from a template; senior engineers reviewed that work, corrected it, and 
in correcting it handed over the judgment the junior could not yet put into words. The junior 
absorbed, almost by osmosis, the thousand small “we don’t do it that way here” lessons that live 
in no document. It was slow, it was expensive, and it was how the profession reproduced itself. 
Every senior engineer was once someone’s costly mistake to tolerate.
Generative AI has quietly voided the bargain. The boilerplate, the well-specified ticket, the small 
bug, the first-pass test: these were the rungs by which juniors climbed, and they are precisely the 
work a large language model now does well, instantly, at the cost of an API call. So the senior 
who once delegated that work downward to a junior now delegates it sideways, to a machine that 
2
needs no mentoring, consumes no headcount, and never has to be promoted. This is not a 
forecast. In a Stanford Digital Economy Lab study of payroll records across millions of workers, 
Brynjolfsson et al.1 find that since generative AI went mainstream, employment for early-career 
workers in the most exposed occupations has fallen about 13 percent relative to their senior 
colleagues, whose employment held steady or grew; for early-career software developers, the 
relative drop approaches 20 percent. The bottom rung is being sawn off. That study is a working 
paper, not yet peer reviewed, and its methods have drawn debate; we treat its magnitudes as 
indicative rather than settled.
It is tempting to read this as the opening act of a familiar story, the one in which the machines 
come for the work, juniors first and seniors next, leaving us to argue only about how many years 
the seniors have left. That story is both wrong and, more to the point, beside the point. The right 
question is not who gets automated but what gets automated. What these systems do 
extraordinarily well is breadth: the conventional pattern, the plausible solution, the survey of 
how a thing is usually done, produced across more languages and frameworks than any person 
will ever hold in their head. What they still do poorly is depth: why this choice is right in this 
system, what failed here three years ago and must never be tried again, which of the plausible 
answers is the correct one. Breadth has become cheap. Depth is still expensive. And depth is 
what tells you whether the cheap breadth is any good.
The trap is this. The work we just handed to the machine was never only production; it was also 
the apprenticeship. It was where depth came from. Automate the breadth and you do not merely 
displace the junior; you sever the channel through which juniors used to become the seniors the 
whole structure still rests on.
Yet the ending is not written. The same Stanford data carries a quieter finding: the employment 
declines cluster where firms used AI to automate work, and largely spare the places that used it 
to augment work. The technology does not pick the equilibrium; the way an organization deploys 
it does. One path (let us call it substitution) uses AI to replace the junior, starve the 
apprenticeship, and let the review of all that machine-made code decay into rubber-stamping; it 
hollows out the pipeline and, in time, the accountability the pipeline existed to produce. The 
other (complementarity) turns AI’s breadth into a faster on-ramp for human depth, and rebuilds 
the apprenticeship in a new form. Which path a company walks is a management decision, not a 
technological fate. Table 1 sets the two paths side by side.

This article is about making that decision well. It is not one more estimate of when the seniors 
run out of road. It traces how the work of software engineering is actually being inverted, junior 
displacement first and a subtler transmutation of senior work after, as the scarce human 
contribution shifts from producing code to verifying and contextualizing it. Then it turns 
practical. It gives engineering leaders a set of moves for steering toward the equilibrium that 
builds a depth pipeline rather than burning it: how to shape machine output so a human can still 
review it, how to keep verification from collapsing into theater, how to rebuild the apprenticeship 
on purpose, and how to measure people by something other than the volume of code that AI has 
made nearly free. We open with the technology in brief, trace the order in which the work is 
falling, and then turn to the argument’s center: the review gate where the inversion succeeds or 
fails, the moves and measures that steer it, a second and less obvious argument for the junior as 
the organization’s source of fresh perspective, the data pipeline where the pattern lands, how to 
forecast where verification will fail, and the conditions under which we are wrong. The 
technology will not decide this. Leaders will.
A note on what this article is. It is a perspective grounded in the empirical record, not a report of 
new measurement, and its contribution is threefold. First, it names review theater, the quiet 
decoupling of an accountable signature from the verification it is meant to certify, as the specific 
mechanism by which the substitution path fails. Second, it extends two settled ideas, the 
exploration-and-exploitation tension in organizational learning and the economics of firmspecific human capital, into the agentic-AI setting, by separating the breadth that machines now 
make cheap from the depth and divergence they do not. Third, it offers moves that are newly 
enforceable rather than merely newly advisable. Others have made the adjacent case that firms 
should keep hiring and deliberately train early-career engineers.36 We take that as established and 
spend our pages on what it leaves open: how verification survives the volume, and why the 
junior is not only a future senior but a present source of divergence.

2. The Technology in Brief
Three pieces of technology drive everything in this article, and a leader needs only their shapes.
The first is the large language model itself: a neural network trained on a vast share of the public 
code ever written, which generates the most probable continuation of whatever it is given. Two 
properties matter here. The output is conventional by construction, because the model is a 
distillation of how things are usually done, which is precisely what we have been calling breadth. 
And the marginal cost is close to zero. The canonical measurement of what that buys: in a 
randomized experiment, Peng et al.2 found that developers working with an AI assistant 
completed a standardized programming task 55.8 percent faster than the control group. The 
effect is not confined to the laboratory: across three field experiments at Microsoft, Accenture, 
and a Fortune 100 company, Cui et al.3 found that developers given an AI coding assistant 
completed roughly 26 percent more tasks, with the largest gains among less-experienced 
developers. The quality of machine-written code is not the headline. The price and the speed are.
The second piece addresses the model’s most obvious gap: it knows the world’s code, not yours. 
Retrieval-augmented generation4 pairs the model with a search layer over an organization’s own 
corpus, the codebase, the documentation, the ticket history, the incident postmortems, and feeds 
the relevant slices into the model’s context before it answers. This is how a generic model 
acquires something like institutional memory. Note the boundary, because the rest of the article 
leans on it: retrieval can surface anything the organization ever wrote down. It cannot surface 
what was never written down, and most of what this article calls depth lives there.
The third piece is the agent: the same model placed in a loop where it can plan, act through tools 
(run the tests, read the logs, propose the change for review), observe the result, and try again. 
That loop pattern5 converts one-shot text generation into multi-step work, and the progress has 
been steep. On SWE-bench, a benchmark built from real GitHub issues6, systems went from 
resolving low single-digit percentages of tasks when the benchmark appeared in late 2023 to a 
large share of them today, per the public leaderboard data7. The exact figure is contested, and we 
treat the most-cited leaderboard numbers with the skepticism they deserve later in this article; 
what is not in question is the direction. Agents now complete real units of engineering work, end 
to end, at machine speed.
Software was always going to be the first profession this reshaped. Code is structured, bound by 
syntax, and, unlike most knowledge work, partially self-verifying: a test suite gives the loop a 
signal to climb. The same properties that made code automatable made it the perfect medium for 
breadth. What none of the three primitives supplies is the rest of the job: knowing which of the 
plausible answers is right for this system, this team, this regulator, this decade. That gap, 
between what the technology can produce and what the organization can trust, is where the entire 
argument that follows takes place.

3. The Order in Which the Work Falls
The inversion does not happen everywhere at once. Work falls to agents in a predictable order, 
governed by three properties: how verifiable the output is, how rich the feedback signal, and how 
expensive a mistake is allowed to be. Bounded output, fast feedback, and low consequence fall 
first. Work entangled with accountability, regulation, and other human beings falls last, if it falls 
at all. Figure 1 places the lifecycle activities along this gradient at a glance, and Table 2 gives the

That ordering explains the past three years. The junior’s portfolio (well-specified 
implementation, tests, first-pass debugging, documentation) sat at the falls-first extreme. Every 
item on it was bounded, checkable, and cheap to get wrong, which is why assistants absorbed it 
almost immediately. That was the first phase, and it is the one most organizations are living in 
now.
6
The senior’s portfolio is falling too, in pieces and in order. Code review goes early, because its 
output is the most bounded of the senior functions (comments and an approval state), and agents 
already draft credible first-pass reviews; that is exactly why the verification question this article 
centers on has become urgent. Debugging and incident triage follow, being iterative, observable, 
and blessed with a clear success signal. Deployment and release management sit further along 
only because rollback makes their mistakes recoverable. Sign-off barely moves at all, because a 
signature is not a technical act; it is an institutional one. Architecture, often defended as the most 
human of the functions, is more exposed than its defenders assume. Most architectural decisions 
are instances of well-known patterns applied to a particular context, and an agent with access to 
the firm’s decision records, cost data, and the pattern literature can propose and evaluate 
candidates credibly. What it cannot do is answer for the choice.
Project the curve forward and the shape of the final phase appears: agents execute most of the 
software development lifecycle, while human work concentrates into specifying what should 
exist, verifying what was built, supplying the context no model holds, and answering for the 
result. The residual is real, but it is smaller than the profession’s self-image. The dates are 
arguable; the direction, we expect, is not. That is why this article spends its remaining pages not 
on the forecast but on the gate where the trajectory either works or fails: review.

4. Review Theater: When Verification Becomes the Bottleneck
For most of the profession’s history, writing code was the scarce step and reviewing it was the 
cheap one. A senior could read far more code in a day than they could write, so review was 
rarely the thing that ran out. Generative AI inverts that ratio. A single engineer directing capable 
models now produces pull requests faster than any human, or any affordable number of humans, 
7
can actually read them. The scarce resource is no longer the capacity to produce code. It is the 
capacity to verify it.
This matters more than it first appears, because review was never only about catching defects. In 
their foundational study of modern code review at Microsoft, Bacchelli and Bird8 found that its 
primary value lies in understanding and knowledge transfer: the reviewer builds a mental model 
of the change, and in doing so carries forward how the system actually works. Defect-finding is 
real but secondary. Review, in other words, is where the apprenticeship lived. It is the same 
channel through which depth was passed down and through which correctness was confirmed, 
and both functions ride on the same act.
That act degrades predictably under load. In their case study of code review at Google, Sadowski 
et al.9 found that reviewers spend most of their effort on comprehension; when a change runs 
past the limit of what they can hold in their head, they fall back from semantic checking to 
syntactic checking. They stop asking whether the logic is right and start checking whether the 
code looks right. The curve is steep. A controlled experiment by di Biase et al.10 found that 
breaking a change into small, single-purpose pieces sharpens the review, with fewer false issues 
and more context-seeking, and the Cisco Systems code-review study reported by Cohen11 put the 
practical ceiling at a few hundred lines, with defect detection falling off sharply beyond it. A 
reviewer handed a thousand-line change is not reviewing it in any meaningful sense. They are 
skimming it.
Now point a breadth machine at that pipeline. An analysis by GitClear12 of 211 million lines of 
changed code written between 2020 and 2024 found that as AI assistance spread, copy-pasted or 
cloned lines rose from 8.3 percent of changed code in 2021 to 12.3 percent in 2024, duplicated 
blocks roughly quadrupled, and the share of changes devoted to refactoring fell from about a 
quarter to under a tenth. The association is correlational rather than causal, but it runs 
consistently across the dataset’s signals and matches what one would expect when a breadth 
machine meets a fixed human review capacity. More code, more nearly-right code, more churn, 
all arriving faster than before, all flowing toward the same fixed human review capacity. The 
breadth side of the ledger scaled. The depth side did not.
The result deserves a name: review theater. The organization still requires a human to approve 
the change, because accountability (and in regulated industries, the law) demands a name on the 
record. Yet the human can no longer actually verify what they are signing. So “looks good to 
me” stops being a judgment and becomes a ritual, because the reviewer no longer has the 
capacity to make it anything else. The signature is still there, carrying its full legal and 
organizational weight, but the verification it is supposed to certify has quietly drained out of it. 
This is the exact failure mode of the substitution path: not a dramatic outage, but an accountable 
gate that still closes while no longer checking anything. Organizational theory long ago named 
the general pattern: Meyer and Rowan37 called it the decoupling of an organization’s formal 
ceremonies from its actual work, the ritual persisting after its substance has gone. What is new 
8
here is the mechanism and the scale: an accountable signature that closes automatically while the 
volume it certifies outruns anyone’s capacity to check it.
Two findings make this worse than ordinary corner-cutting. First, the agent’s output is 
confidently wrong in non-obvious ways. When Yu et al.13 re-examined the patches that leading 
coding agents had marked as solved on SWE-bench Verified, nearly one in five proved 
semantically incorrect, passing the provided test while failing the real task; under their 
strengthened tests the top agent’s score fell from 78.8 to 62.2 percent. Second, the human verifier 
is systematically overconfident about their own speed and accuracy. In a randomized controlled 
trial, METR14 found that experienced open-source developers worked about 19 percent slower 
with AI assistance on their own mature codebases, even as they believed they had been sped up 
by roughly the same margin. The same overconfidence appears in quality, not only speed: in a 
controlled study, Perry et al.15 found that developers using an AI assistant wrote less secure code 
than those without one, while being more likely to believe their code was secure. Put those 
together. The agent looks more reliable than it is, the reviewer feels more effective than they are, 
and the gap between the feeling and the fact is exactly where the unverified change slips through.
This is why review theater is the hinge of the argument rather than a mere annoyance. It hollows 
out the one function we have argued is most durable, accountable human verification, and it does 
so invisibly. Velocity rises. The dashboard stays green. The depth that review used to transmit 
stops being transmitted, the correctness it used to certify stops being certified, and nothing on the 
surface says so until something breaks in production with a human name attached to the change 
that caused it. An organization can saw through its own verification capacity this way without 
ever deciding to.
The response is not to tell reviewers to try harder, which only postpones the failure, and it is not 
to trust the machine, which is the substitution equilibrium by another name. It is to redesign the 
work so that human depth and machine breadth meet on terms a person can actually sustain: 
shrink the unit of review until it fits in a human head again, and match the depth of review to the 
consequence of the change. We turn to those moves next.
5. Two Moves: Shrink the Unit, Tier the Scrutiny
The remedy follows directly from the diagnosis. If review theater sets in when breadth arrives 
faster than depth can absorb it, then only two levers are worth pulling: the size of the thing being 
reviewed, and the amount of human judgment applied to it. The first shrinks the unit until a 
person can actually hold it. The second spends scarce human depth where it matters and 
conserves it where it does not.
The First Move: Shrink the Unit of Review
Start with the unit. The single most effective move available to an engineering organization is to 
require that change arrive in small, single-responsibility pull requests, one concern per change, 
9
each carrying the reasoning behind it. The evidence for this predates the AI era and is unusually 
consistent. A controlled experiment by di Biase et al.16 showed that decomposing a change into 
focused pieces measurably sharpens the review that follows; the Cisco Systems study reported 
by Cohen17 put the effective ceiling at a few hundred lines, beyond which defect detection 
collapses; and Sadowski et al.18 documented that small changes are the norm at Google precisely 
because they are the ones humans can review well. The AI era has only sharpened the case for it: 
the 2024 Accelerate State of DevOps report19 found that AI adoption raised individual developer 
productivity while reducing software delivery throughput and stability, and named small batch 
sizes as a core discipline for containing the risk. None of this is new. What is new is who writes 
the code. In the old world you asked human juniors to keep their pull requests small, and they 
often did not, because decomposition is tedious and the incentives ran the other way. Now the 
producer is an agent you instruct, and atomicity becomes a property you can simply require of its 
output. The reviewer’s lost capacity is handed back by reshaping the producer around the 
reviewer, rather than the reverse.
What is genuinely new here is worth isolating, because “write smaller pull requests” is old 
advice. The novelty is that the constraint has become enforceable. You could never reliably make 
human juniors decompose their work; you can require single-responsibility output from an agent 
and reject at the gate anything that violates it, the way a build fails on a broken test. That 
enforceability does not depend on the model choosing to comply. It rides on deterministic 
scaffolding outside the model, the same mechanism the reliability discussion below recommends 
for everything an agent cannot be trusted to get right on its own.
This is not a brake on speed, which is the objection it usually draws. The breadth machine can 
still run flat out. Atomic decomposition changes only the shape in which its output arrives, so 
that volume and verifiability stop competing. You get both: the throughput of the machine, and 
review that still means something.
The Second Move: Tier the Scrutiny to the Cost of Being Wrong
The second move is to stop reviewing everything the same way, because not all change carries 
the same cost of being wrong.
When Asynchronous Review Suffices
Most change is low in consequence and easily reversed, and there an asynchronous, agentassisted review is entirely adequate; the agent can even take the first pass and flag what a human 
should look at.
When a Human Must Lead
High-consequence change is different in kind, not degree. A modification to a security boundary, 
a data schema, an irreversible migration, anything that touches money or safety, anything that in 
a regulated domain carries an accountable signature, demands a human-led review. The 
argument here is precise. It is not that asynchronous review is bad. It is that asynchronous-by-

For that high-consequence tier, synchronous and collective review (people reasoning aloud 
together, in a room or its remote equivalent) does two things asynchronous approval cannot. It 
makes rubber-stamping difficult, because a person cannot quietly wave through what they are 
required to discuss. And it reopens the channel that Bacchelli and Bird20 identified as the real 
value of review in the first place: understanding, transferred from one engineer to another. This 
is where the apprenticeship returns. A junior who watches a senior interrogate a consequential 
change is acquiring judgment the way the apprentice did, learning which stone will hold by 
watching the master reject the ones that will not. The agent supplies the volume. The 
synchronous review supplies the depth, and with it, quietly, the making of the next senior.
The principle underneath both moves is this: let the machine produce at the speed of breadth, 
then force its output back into the units and forums where human depth can still reach it. Simple 
to state, hard to live by. Shrinking the unit protects verification on the way in. Tiering the 
scrutiny spends judgment where being wrong is costly. Together they turn the volume that 
produced review theater into something a person can still stand behind.
These two moves defend verification at the level of the individual change. They leave open the 
larger questions of measurement and staffing: what to count once lines of code no longer mean 
anything, and how to keep a pipeline of future seniors alive after the junior’s old work has gone 
to the machine. We turn to those shortly. First, the junior deserves a second argument, one that 
has nothing to do with their future.

6. The Junior as a Source of Divergence
Experience is depth, and this article has treated depth as the scarce and valuable thing. Depth has 
a shadow, though, and it is worth naming. The senior is anchored twice over: to the 
organization’s accumulated orthodoxy, the long catalogue of things “we tried and they did not 
work” and “this is how it is done here,” and to their own hardened priors, the patterns that have 
served them well and that they have therefore stopped questioning. Some of that anchoring is 
precisely the judgment we want to keep. Some of it is merely age in the system, a settled set of 
assumptions that the world may already have moved past.
The intuitive correction is to reach for the machine, on the theory that a model trained on the 
whole internet must surely widen the aperture. It does the opposite. A large language model is 
built to return the most probable continuation, which is to say the consensus of its training data, 
and alignment training narrows the range further. Padmakumar and He21 found that writing with 
a feedback-tuned model measurably reduced the diversity of what people produced, an effect 
they traced to the model’s own contributed text. At the level of a population the effect 
compounds: Doshi and Hauser22 found that AI-assisted stories were judged more creative 
individually while growing more similar to one another, and Anderson et al.23 found that 
different people working with the same model converged on less distinct ideas. Most of these 
measurements come from writing and ideation, but the pattern shows up in code as well: Moradi 
Dakhel et al.24 found that GitHub Copilot tends to reproduce the same or very similar solutions 
across attempts, and that the developer most exposed to the homogenized output is the novice 
who lacks the depth to filter it. The mechanism is not domain-bound. The senior who answers 
entrenchment by consulting an LLM is not opening a window. They are installing a louder 
version of the room they are already standing in.
We should be plain about how far this evidence reaches. Most of it comes from writing and 
ideation, not from software architecture, and the study closest to code, Moradi Dakhel and 
colleagues on the sameness of a coding assistant’s suggestions, is suggestive rather than decisive. 
So the claim that a senior-plus-agent core structurally lacks divergence is a hypothesis, not a 
finding. We advance it because the mechanism is general and the cost of being wrong about it is 
high, and we state plainly in the risks section what would overturn it: evidence that AI-assisted 
engineering does not, in fact, narrow the range of what organizations build. What we do not 
claim is that this homogenization has already been measured in engineering decisions at the level 
of a team or a firm. That measurement is the study this argument most needs, and we would 
revise if it came back the other way.
Nor does the obvious hedge, reaching for several models at once, escape the problem. Huh et 
al.25 argue that as models grow they converge toward a shared internal representation of the 
world. If they are right, the practical inference is ours but hard to avoid: a committee of frontier 
models is closer to one opinion in several accents than to a genuine spread of view. The senior 
directing a fleet of agents is, structurally, running a convergence engine. It is superb at producing 
12
the conventional answer and constitutionally blind to the one that nobody in the training 
distribution thought to write down.
This is the second reason the junior matters, distinct from the apprenticeship argument and in 
some ways more immediate. The tension is an old one, what March26 called the balance of 
exploration against exploitation: a senior-plus-agent core is a superb engine for exploiting the 
patterns it already knows and a poor explorer of the ones it does not, and an organization that 
loses its explorers drifts toward the institutional groupthink Janis27 anatomized. Precisely because 
the junior lacks the organization’s priors and the senior’s scar tissue, they are the one party in the 
system still capable of divergence. They ask the naive question that turns out to be the loadbearing one. They propose the approach the organization rejected years ago, before the constraint 
that killed it was quietly lifted. They have not yet learned what everyone else has agreed to stop 
seeing. This is not a charming side effect of inexperience, to be tolerated until the junior is 
properly socialized. It is a distinct and increasingly scarce input, and socializing it away is a loss, 
not a graduation.
The complementarity sharpens accordingly. The productive unit is not senior plus LLM, which is 
depth plus breadth and both of them convergent. It is senior plus LLM plus junior: depth, 
breadth, and divergence, with the third supplying exactly what the first two structurally cannot. 
An organization that sheds its juniors to let seniors run agent fleets does not only starve its future 
pipeline, the harm named earlier. It removes its one live source of divergence at the very moment 
it powers up the convergence engine, and it will get what that design produces: output that is 
faster, more confident, more uniform, and progressively closed to whatever lies outside the 
consensus it has now automated.
7. What to Measure, and Whom to Hire
The two moves so far protect verification on a single change. Two questions remain, and they are 
settled at the level of the organization rather than the pull request: what the firm measures, and 
whom it hires. Both are usually decided by inertia, and inertia now points toward substitution. A 
leader who wants the other equilibrium has to choose it deliberately, against the default.
Start with measurement. Engineering organizations have long counted output: lines of code, 
commits, pull requests merged, story points, issues closed. These measures were always crude, 
but they tracked value loosely, because producing code was slow and expensive and so more of 
it usually meant more work done. Agents sever that link. When code is nearly free to produce, 
output metrics measure the cost that has collapsed rather than the value that remains, and they do 
something worse: they reward the precise behavior that manufactures review theater, more code, 
more pull requests, more churn, a greener dashboard. To measure output in an agentic 
organization is to pay people for running the substitution equilibrium.
The measures that survive are the ones that track what has become scarce. Three kinds are worth 
naming. The first is verified outcome: not how much was produced, but how much of it proved

Then staffing. The substitution equilibrium is seductive on paper: a junior is slower and costlier 
per unit of code than the agent that has absorbed the junior’s old work, so the move that looks 
rational is to stop hiring juniors and let seniors direct fleets. The two preceding arguments are 
what that arithmetic leaves out. The junior was never only a junior. They were the organization’s 
pipeline of future seniors, and its one live source of divergence against a senior-plus-LLM core 
that converges by construction. Remove them and both losses are real, deferred, and invisible, 
which is precisely why a spreadsheet will keep recommending it, until the day the firm finds it 
has no seniors left to promote and no one positioned to notice the consensus has gone wrong.
The deliberate move is to rebuild the apprenticeship on purpose, because it will no longer arrive 
as a free byproduct of cheap junior work. Its new shape is already implied by the moves above: 
put juniors on real problems as directors of agents rather than writers of boilerplate; route their 
work through the synchronous review of consequential changes, where senior judgment is 
exercised in the open and can be watched; and protect the moments when their inexperience 
produces a divergent idea, instead of training it out of them. The hard-won knowledge the 
organization accumulates along the way, the failure modes and the reasons behind “we do not do 
it that way here,” is worth writing down as explicit, evidence-backed guardrails, which then do 
14
double duty: they constrain the agents and they teach the newcomers. What once happened on its 
own, at the price of tolerating slow junior code, now has to be designed and funded on purpose.
Measurement and staffing are, in the end, the same decision asked twice. Measure output and 
hire no juniors, and the organization has optimized itself into the substitution equilibrium: fast, 
cheap, convergent, and hollow. Measure verified outcome, depth, and divergence, and staff to 
produce them, and it has built the complementarity equilibrium instead, the one in which the 
machine’s breadth is governed by human depth and refreshed by human divergence. The 
technology in the two firms is identical. What differs is the metric and the org chart, and both are 
chosen, not given.
These are the levers a leader actually holds. Two tasks remain: to watch the whole pattern land in 
one concrete domain, and then to ask how much confidence the trajectory deserves. The domain 
comes first. We turn to those next.
8. The Case of the Enterprise Data Pipeline
Abstractions persuade no one who runs a budget, so we ground the argument in a setting most 
enterprise organizations will recognize: the complex data pipeline. We choose it deliberately. 
Data engineering concentrates every element the argument needs: real architectural complexity, 
heavy reliance on tacit institutional knowledge, frequent production incidents with crossfunctional consequences, regulatory exposure (personal data, financial reporting, health records), 
and an unusually clean split between high-judgment design work and high-volume 
implementation work. It is also the domain where agents are advancing fastest, because the 
artifacts (SQL, configuration, schemas, lineage graphs) are highly structured and the feedback 
signals (data quality, freshness, cost) are quantitative and observable. Breadth machines love 
structure, and a pipeline is structure all the way down.
A representative pipeline draws from dozens or hundreds of sources and lands them on a 
platform of object storage, a processing engine, an orchestrator, and a transformation layer, 
feeding regulatory reports, executive dashboards, model training, and customer analytics. The 
data must arrive on time, reflect its sources, remain traceable, and comply with whatever applies 
(GDPR, HIPAA, SOX), at a tolerable cost. The old division of labor maps onto the line this 
article has drawn. The senior owned the depth: schema-evolution contracts, the canonical model, 
the data-quality framework, the lineage stack, incident command, the architecture review, the 
release signature. The junior owned the breadth: transformation logic, tests, templated 
orchestration, documentation, dashboards, first-pass debugging, directed backfills. The junior’s 
economic role was to multiply the senior; the developmental role was to become one.

In practice the daily texture changes more than the org chart. Schema design becomes a dialogue 
the human signs off on; pipeline construction collapses toward stating intent and letting the agent 
produce the models, tests, and documentation in reviewable units; quality assertions are inferred 
from production data and proposed for approval; and when the pipeline fails at two in the 
morning, the agent has already correlated the logs and drafted a fix before the human chooses 
among options and faces the downstream teams. Every one has the same shape: breadth 
proposed by the machine, depth applied by a person, with review tiered to the cost of being 
wrong. A backfill on an internal dashboard is not a schema change under SOX, and the firm that 
reviews them identically has missed the point.
The implication this article keeps returning to is sharpest here. Senior data engineers are made by 
exposure: weird data, idiosyncratic sources, partial failures, the small population of incidents that 
teach the deep failure modes of distributed systems. Those exposures live exactly in the rows the 
agents take. A firm can therefore automate its pipeline and, without ever deciding to, stop 
manufacturing the people who can run one. The entry role that replaces the old one is a director 
of agentic data systems who specifies intent, maintains the evaluation harness, audits the lineage 
and quality assertions, intervenes where agent confidence and reality part company, and supplies 
the question nobody else will ask. Staffed and reviewed as this article proposes, the pipeline 
becomes the complementarity equilibrium in miniature. Left to default, it becomes the 
substitution equilibrium with excellent dashboards.
9. Forecasting Where Verification Fails
The metrics proposed in Section 7 tell an organization whether verification is holding after the 
fact. Leaders also need to see where it is about to break. We offer a way, and we are explicit that 
it is a framework to be tested, not a validated instrument: the escaped-defect data that would 
confirm it does not yet exist, because the agentic era is too young to have produced it. What the 
framework supplies now is a set of observable leading indicators and a stated test.
17
Whether a machine-made change is genuinely verified is governed less by the seniority of the 
person steering the model than by how they use it. Verification is a practice, not a rank. Five 
behaviors set the exposure of any given change. Specification: how completely intent and 
constraints are stated before generation, since a thin prompt instructs the model to invent the 
unstated requirements, plausibly and often wrongly. Scope: the size of the unit requested, since a 
whole-feature ask returns more than a reviewer can hold in their head, which is the shrink-theunit discipline restated as a risk. Grounding: whether the model is anchored in the actual 
codebase and constraints or left to answer from the average of its training data. Interrogation: 
whether the person challenges the output, asks for the edge cases, and demands to know why this 
answer is right in this system, or accepts the first plausible response. Verification loop: whether 
deterministic checks surround the generation, or correctness rides entirely on human inspection, 
which Section 4 shows is systematically overconfident.
A change's verification exposure is where it sits across these five, and the distribution of 
exposure across a team's change stream is the quantity a leader should watch. It sorts people into 
usage archetypes that have nothing to do with tenure (Figure 2). The engineer who specifies 
tightly, keeps the unit small, and wraps the work in evaluations runs low exposure whatever their 
title; the one who accepts large, ungrounded generations on inspection runs high exposure 
whatever theirs. Two intermediate types are common and deceptive: the grounded truster, who 
prompts well and reads carefully but builds no automated check, and the volume user, who 
directs the model faster than anyone can examine, so that exposure rises not per change but in 
aggregate as the unreviewed remainder grows. We do not claim these five drivers are 
independent, or that they compose into a single score by simple addition; Figure 2 arranges them 
along two summary axes as a typology, and turning it into a measured index is part of the work 
we propose.
18
Figure 2. A hypothesized typology, not a measured instrument. Verification exposure is posited to rise as usage

We deliberately leave open whether seniority correlates with low exposure. It is tempting to 
assume that experienced engineers, holding the system's failure modes in memory, are the ones 
who interrogate the model and build the loops. It is equally possible that experience breeds overtrust, the automation bias that oversight regimes are built to counter, and that a junior trained 
explicitly on verification outperforms a senior who has stopped checking. We do not have the 
data to settle this, and we decline to assert intuition as a finding. The management implication 
does not depend on the answer: if verification is a practice, it can be taught, measured, and 
required, which is what the apprenticeship and measurement moves of the preceding sections are 
for. This does not contradict routing consequential review to senior-led forums: that assignment 
reflects accountability and hard-won domain judgment for high-stakes calls, not a claim that 
seniors verify better on average. The point is that verification is a practice to be taught and 
measured across levels, not a property of rank.
The framework's value is that it can be instrumented before the defect data arrives. Every driver 
has a proxy an organization already collects: scope appears as change size, the verification loop 
as test and gate coverage, grounding and interrogation in tool and session logs, provenance in 
how reliably a change carries its reasoning. An organization can build an exposure index today 
19
from signals it already has, watch it move, and validate it later by correlating exposure with the 
incidents the coming years will supply. That is the honest form of a forecast: not a prediction 
dressed as data, but a measurable hypothesis with a stated test.
Some of these indicators are already visible in practice, in a vignette we offer as illustration 
rather than validation. In one large enterprise engineering organization, we compared several 
thousand commits across a group of related product repositories, spanning a multi-year window 
before and a several-month window after LLM coding assistants first appeared in their commit 
history, against an older and larger infrastructure codebase that carried no such marker and 
serves as a null control. Counting all commits, weekly volume rose about two-and-a-half times; 
counting only human-authored non-merge commits, the merge-adjusted figure, it rose about 1.8 
times, so a leader watching the raw number would have overstated the change in real work by 
roughly a third. The pull-request-merge share of the stream more than doubled, all of it humanauthored. Commit-to-ticket traceability fell by about a quarter, and the fall was sharpest in the 
teams that had been most disciplined before. The share of commits carrying tests diluted even as 
their absolute number held, and no rework spike appeared. The control codebase showed none of 
these shifts. The comparison is correlational, the adoption boundary is a lagging lower bound, 
and we draw it from a single organization; we report it to show what the exposure indicators look 
like when they move, not to establish a general finding.
Propositions and research agenda. To make the argument usable and falsifiable, we state its 
core as testable propositions and note what would confirm or refute each. P1: as machinegenerated change scales, the binding constraint on software quality shifts from the capacity to 
produce code to the capacity to verify it. P2: verification failure, what we call review theater, 
rises with the volume of machine-generated change relative to fixed human review capacity, 
largely independent of the models' peak capability. P3: an organization's verification exposure is 
governed by how its people use these tools (specification, scope, grounding, interrogation, and 
evaluation loops) rather than by their seniority. P4: organizations that sustain early-career hiring 
and deliberate apprenticeship retain more depth and divergence, and lower long-run verification 
exposure, than those that substitute. Each is testable with evidence organizations already hold or 
soon will, and together they form the research agenda this article invites.
10. Risks and Conditions of Reversal
An argument should be able to say what would slow it, stall it, or prove it wrong. This one can. 
Three risks deserve extended treatment, two more deserve mention, and we close with the 
conditions under which the thesis itself fails.
The Organizational Risk: Incentives, Not Villains
The first risk is organizational, and it is best stated without villains. The managers who govern 
adoption are accountable under the old rules: predictable delivery, controlled risk, the signature 
on the release. Under those accountabilities, slowing an unproven and non-deterministic 
20
technology is not obstruction. It is the job, performed as currently defined. The inertia the 
transition meets is therefore not primarily a defense of turf; it is the old measurement system 
defending itself through the people who are paid on it. The dynamics are well documented, from 
the standard catalogue of reasons people resist change28 to the way incumbents' resourceallocation processes filter out investments that do not serve today’s customers and metrics29. The 
cure is the one this article has already proposed. Change what the middle of the organization is 
measured on before asking it to behave differently.
The same incentives produce a second pattern that looks like the opposite of resistance and is the 
same mistake. Call it adoption theater: the organization mandates AI use and measures it by 
consumption, tokens spent, the share of code written by machine, seats licensed. This recommits 
the output-metric error at the adoption layer, and it produces two recognizable endings. In one, 
the firm measures usage, finds no value (it was never measuring value), and reverts wholesale, 
concluding that AI failed when only the measurement did. In the other, it doubles down and 
removes the humans whose verification made the output trustworthy, executing the substitution 
equilibrium at speed. The markers are visible in advance: usage targets without outcome metrics, 
pilot whiplash, mandates unaccompanied by any change to what is measured or to how review 
works. None of this is misconduct; it is what an unchanged incentive structure produces, 
whoever stands inside it. Nothing here is an argument against management. Tiered review, 
rebuilt apprenticeships, and outcome metrics are run by managers. Huy30 documented that 
transformations tend to live or die in the middle layer, and this one is no exception; like the 
senior engineer’s, the manager’s role transmutes rather than disappears.
The Technological Risk: Capability Versus Reliability
The second risk is technological, and it is two claims usually argued as one. Capability is what a 
model does at its best. Reliability is what it does on the thousandth unattended run. Capability 
has risen steeply; the benchmark trajectory cited earlier is real. Reliability is where the ceiling 
lives, because a language model is a sampled distribution rather than an instructed employee: 
every guideline it is given will, at some frequency, be ignored. Guardrails lower that frequency. 
Nothing yet eliminates it.
If the ceiling holds, the answer is not to argue with the model but to contain it, and the required 
discipline is one engineering already owns: building reliable systems from unreliable parts. 
Deterministic scaffolding outside the model (tests, schemas, typed interfaces, automated gates) 
catches what instruction alone cannot, and human judgment covers the residual that the 
scaffolding cannot see. The junior engineer was a non-deterministic component too, and code 
review and the apprenticeship were the guardrail system the profession built around them; the 
agent does not break that tradition so much as rejoin it. The point that matters for this article is 
the asymmetry. A reliability plateau slows the later phases of the inversion, but it makes the 
verification bottleneck more binding, not less. The plateau scenario strengthens the case for 
everything proposed here.
21
The Pipeline Risk: Who Trains the Next Senior
The third risk is the one this article is about, operating one level up. Training juniors has always 
produced what Becker31 called general human capital: transferable skill that walks out the door 
when its owner does, which is why competitive firms underinvest in it and free-ride on one 
another to supply it. Yet the same junior work is where the firm-specific depth this article 
emphasizes is also built, so automating it starves both at once: the transferable skill a firm never 
chose to fund and the specific depth it did. The early-career contraction already visible in payroll 
data32 suggests the free-riding has begun. The bill arrives mid-transition, as a shortage of exactly 
the seniors and supervisors the agentic organization requires, and it will be paid even by firms 
that believed every word written here but could not hire judgment that no one had trained. The 
mitigation is unsentimental: treat the junior pipeline as strategic capacity rather than goodwill, 
because the firms that train through this period will own the market for depth at the end of it.
A reader versed in the economics of automation will object that this fear is perennial and usually 
wrong. Autor33 shows that automation has historically complemented labor as often as it replaced 
it, raising productivity and demand in ways that created new work, and Bessen34 traces how 
automation first boosted employment in industry after industry while demand was elastic, 
turning to losses only once demand was satiated. The objection is correct as economic history 
and beside the point here. Our claim is not that aggregate software employment must fall; it may 
well rise. It is narrower and harder to escape: the specific channel through which a firm grows its 
own seniors runs through exactly the tasks now being automated, and no buoyant external labor 
market refills an internal pipeline a firm has stopped operating. Automation may add jobs across 
the economy and still leave an individual organization unable to staff the judgment its own 
agents require.
Two further brakes deserve mention. Regulation in accountable domains (finance, healthcare, 
safety-critical systems) will keep humans in the loop longer than the technology alone would 
dictate. That is a brake on pace rather than direction: the emerging regimes mandate oversight 
rather than prohibition, and Article 14 of the EU AI Act is explicit, requiring that high-risk 
systems be designed for effective human oversight and naming automation bias, the tendency to 
over-rely on machine output, as precisely the thing overseers must guard against35. In this case 
the brake reinforces rather than undermines the verification argument. And the socialcoordination floor (the negotiation, alignment, and stakeholder work that runs through human 
institutions) shows no sign of automating; its true size is uncertain and may be larger than this 
article assumes.
Finally, the conditions under which we are simply wrong. If agentic systems demonstrate 
sustained, near-perfect reliability on consequential tasks, audited rather than self-reported, the 
verification bottleneck dissolves and the human role contracts much further than we have argued. 
If entry-level hiring rebounds even as automation-mode adoption deepens, the displacement 
premise weakens. If AI-assisted engineering proves not to narrow the diversity of what 
organizations produce, the divergence argument loses its force. We do not expect these 
22
outcomes. We name them because a forecast should say where it breaks, and this article is meant 
to be used, not believed.
11. Conclusion
The argument of this article can be carried in one hand. Generative and agentic AI have made 
breadth, the production of plausible and conventional work, nearly free, and the first casualty 
was the junior engineer, whose old tasks were breadth almost by definition. Depth (the judgment 
that tells you which plausible answer is right here) and divergence (the fresh sight that notices 
what the consensus cannot) did not get cheaper. They got scarcer, because the channels that 
produced them, the apprenticeship and the unsocialized newcomer, are being dismantled by 
default. The senior is not next in line for the junior’s fate; the senior’s work transmutes, from 
producing code to verifying and contextualizing what machines propose. Whether that 
transmutation lands in a hollowed organization or a stronger one is not a property of the 
technology. It is a sequence of management decisions, most of which are being made right now, 
many of them by omission.
The profession has remade itself before: mainframe to client-server, on-premises to cloud, 
waterfall to agile, monolith to services. Each transition repriced a generation’s skills, and each 
time the engineers and leaders who fared best were the ones who saw the change plainly and 
invested early in what would compound. What is different now is the location of the disruption. 
Previous transitions changed what engineers built with. This one automates the very work 
through which engineers were made. Continuity used to be a free byproduct of doing business. 
Now it is a line item, and someone has to choose to pay it.
The pattern is not unique to software, either. Any profession that made its experts by having 
novices do the simple, checkable work under supervision, law with its associates, medicine with 
its residents, audit and accounting with their juniors, meets the same problem the moment a 
machine can do that simple work well: the output is replaced, and the apprenticeship that was 
hidden inside it goes too. The particulars differ by field. The management question does not. 
Either the pipeline and the verification are left to decay by default, or they are rebuilt on purpose.
The choices are not mysterious, and this article has tried to make them concrete: shrink the unit 
of review, tier scrutiny by consequence, measure what is scarce rather than what is now free, 
keep hiring and training juniors, capture hard-won lessons as guardrails that bind agents and 
teach people, and watch for the two theaters, review and adoption, that look like progress on a 
dashboard and fail only on contact with an incident. Table 6 collects them in one place.

The mason’s apprentice learned the stone by cutting it. That way of making judgment is ending, 
and no amount of nostalgia will restart it. Judgment itself is not ending. It is moving to the center 
of the profession, and the organizations that build the new way of making it, deliberately, while

know which stone will hold. Build the place where they learn it.
