A Multi-Layer Framework for Evaluating the Return on Investment of AI Projects
Enterprise AI spending is projected to exceed $1.8 trillion by 2030, yet more than 80 percent of AI projects fail to deliver measurable returns. Traditional capital budgeting tools were designed for investments with well-defined cash flows and fail to account for the unique risk characteristics of AI: deeply uncertain cost structures, indirect and non-linear benefits, path-dependent value creation, and acute sensitivity to organizational readiness. This paper proposes a multi-layer framework that integrates four complementary analytical lenses into a coherent AI investment decision process: a SCALE strategic readiness assessment (Layer 0), ROIC decomposition through a value driver tree (Layer 1), stage-gate phased investment (Layer 2), and a synthesized decision scorecard (Layer 3). We complement these layers with a novel eight-category risk-adjustment table that systematically adjusts the discount rate to reflect AI-specific risks, including vendor and platform dependency and organizational friction, two dimensions that existing frameworks ignore. We validate the framework against eight real-world AI deployments spanning four deployment categories: B2B (Salesforce Agentforce, validated against Palantir), B2C (Spotify, validated against Duolingo), Platform (Shopify Magic, validated against Uber), and Internal (JPMorgan COiN, validated against NatWest). The framework's contribution is not a new financial formula; it is a structured methodology that forces decision-makers to systematically confront the full range of factors determining AI project outcomes, in an environment where the dominant failure mode is not bad technology but unexamined assumptions.
Who Trains the Next Senior? Managing Software Engineering When AI Makes Code Cheap
Generative and agentic AI have made the routine work of software engineering nearly free, and the first casualty is the junior engineer. Engineering leaders now face a choice most are making by default. Cheap machine-made code commoditizes breadth, the plausible and conventional, while raising the value of two things humans still supply and machines do not: depth, the judgment of which answer is right in this system, and divergence, the perspective from outside the consensus that a senior-plus-AI team structurally lacks. Left to inertia, organizations slide into substitution, replacing juniors and letting review, apprenticeship, and the diversity of what they build hollow out. The alternative, complementarity, is a set of concrete moves: shrink the unit of review, tier scrutiny by consequence, measure verified outcomes rather than raw output, and rebuild the apprenticeship on purpose. We show why the choice is real, what each path costs, and what to do about it now.
Drone-Megaddon: A Real-Time Strategy Interface for Managing Drone Swarms
Neural sensors, Oculus Rift, mobile devices - to date, drone researchers have utilized a variety of diﬀerent media to control a single drone. However, to the best of our knowledge, there are currently no adequate solutions for dealing with multiple drones at a time. So, in this paper, we describe Drone-Megaddon, an Android-based real-time strategy interface patterned after the hit RTS game StarCraft II, which facilitates high-level management of a drone swarm.
Drone-Megaddon 2026: Co-Intelligent Swarm Control with Language and Vision Models
In 2014, we introduced Drone-Megaddon, an Android interface for managing drone swarms, patterned after real time strategy (RTS) video games like StarCraft II. We argued that its select-then-execute paradigm fit commanding multiple drones better than the single drone piloting interfaces available at the time. Twelve years later, three things around it have changed. Commercial drones now ship with LTE connectivity and with navigation modes built for environments where GPS is denied. Drone warfare at scale has generated a public body of operational lessons on electronic warfare and operator load. And foundation models have made natural language intent and visual grounding tractable as interfaces to robotic systems. The third change is the one this paper turns on. We propose a successor to Drone-Megaddon that keeps the 2014 core: RTS selection, the $RAD command queue, ACK’d radio, and human oversight. Over that core we propose three AI layers. The intent translator takes an operator utterance together with the currently selected drones and the map state, and emits a validated sequence of $RAD commands. The perception grounder takes a referring expression such as “follow the red sedan” together with the drone’s current video feed, and emits a tracked bounding box bound to a specific drone. The coordination planner takes a single waypoint intended for multiple drones and emits one distinct final position per drone, addressing what the 2014 paper called the random offset at destination behavior. We describe each layer at the level of its interface, walk through a search-and-rescue example, and address ethical considerations the 2014 paper did not engage with. This is a proposal; the detailed architecture is in §5.
